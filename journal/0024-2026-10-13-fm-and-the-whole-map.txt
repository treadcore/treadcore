            FM, AND EVERY SOUND IN ITS PLACE ON THE WHOLE MAP

                     Paul Richeson, treadcore.org
                           13 October 2026


ABSTRACT

    Journal 0023's sounds were filtered noise and sines. This session
    rebuilt them as two-operator FM after Paul's notes. Direct fire is
    a gunshot (an inharmonic crack whose index falls in milliseconds,
    over a punch swept from 1 kHz to 50 Hz), the indirect gun a cannon
    blast, a direct hit a bell of armour at 1:1.4, and a strike drone a
    missile whose index rises. Then come the machine around the player:
    the breech's doo-cha-klank, the loader, a clock, the
    out-of-ammunition beep, a beating alarm and the tracks' squeal. That
    is 32 cues in three takes each. Placement changed to Paul's model:
    full strength anywhere on screen, panned across it, fading past the
    edge to a floor of 6 %, never silent, low-passed far off. A sound
    whose place the fog hides plays from nowhere, as background. The
    engine runs at 88.2 kHz under Carson's bound and is band-limited to
    44.1 kHz.

INDEX TERMS

    FM synthesis, Carson's rule, oversampling, spatial audio.


I. INTRODUCTION

    Paul sent the FM equation, its Bessel sidebands and recipes for
    weapons, then a second page for machine noises and for mixing many
    voices, and asked that every sound have a place with the whole map
    audible.

II. BACKGROUND

    Journal 0023: 18 cues, placed by distance from the view's centre,
    silent past 120 tiles, interface cues unplaced; 22.05 kHz.

III. METHOD

    1. One operator, `Fm`: y = sin(2π·fc·t + I·sin(2π·fc·ratio·t)),
       phase kept so the frequencies can sweep. Tested against the
       Bessel function: the carrier's share is J0(I), 0.765 at I = 1
       and about zero at I = 2.405, J0's first zero.
    2. The recipes as written: inharmonic ratios for metal and machine
       (1:1.41, 1:1.4, 1:2.7, 1:1.5), harmonic 1:1 for the interface,
       the index envelope as the sound (fast fall: strike; slow rise:
       whoosh).
    3. Nyquist. The machine-noise page's clicks put carriers at 9 kHz
       with modulators at 13.5 kHz; at 22.05 kHz that is all aliasing.
       The rate went to 44.1 kHz and the index is capped by Carson's
       rule. Capped at 44.1 kHz, the klank's index of 6 fell to
       nothing and the cha's 4 to 1. Taken instead, the notes' own
       remedy: the engine runs at twice the rate and a 63-tap Blackman
       windowed sinc band-limits it at 20 kHz before every second sample
       is kept. Sidebands between 22 and 44 kHz are filtered out, not
       folded; the doo keeps its full index of 5.
    4. Placement as Paul put it: the screen's rectangle from the camera,
       the zoom and the window. Inside it, gain 1 and the pan from the
       left edge to the right (nine pan positions, so a sound crossing
       the screen moves with it). Outside, gain falls with the distance
       past the edge, half way to the floor at 20 tiles; past 15 tiles
       the far take, low-passed twice at 1.2 kHz.
    5. The fog. The sim tells every player of an intercept or a downed
       drone but not where, if the drone was out of sight, and the
       sound must not tell it either: it plays centred, at the floor,
       from the far take.
    6. Mixing, from the notes: 6 dB of headroom on the master, each new
       voice scaled by 1/√(1 + playing/8), and the repeat rule changed
       so a far gun cannot mask a nearer one 30 ms later.

IV. CHRONOLOGY

     1. Step 9 committed and pushed (988ed6e).
     2. FM weapons: gun, cannon, strike, ricochet, rocket, rocket hit,
        crash, reload, static, thud, notice; three takes per cue. The
        first gunshot was a thump (9 % of its energy above 2.5 kHz):
        the punch over the crack. Re-weighted: 46 %.
     3. Placement on the screen's rectangle, the floor, the far take.
        An 8-player match played two sounds in 75 s: the fogged ones
        waited for a place that never came and were dropped. With the
        fog rule, 31 sounds from 15 cues in 80 s.
     4. Machine noises. Track: squeal at 3 kHz over a modulator at
        300 Hz, its index wandering 9 ± 5 with noise low-passed at
        6 Hz, gated by slower noise, over a 50 to 200 Hz rumble, the
        loop's seam crossfaded; its volume eased to the tank's speed.
        The first mix buried the squeal (5 % above 2.5 kHz); raised to
        15 %. The loader's ratchet was under its punch; raised.
     5. Carson's cap at 44.1 kHz, then the oversampling (III.3).
     6. An 80-second, 8-player match: 32 sounds, the breech after the
        seat's own shots, the alarm at low armour, the fogged intercepts
        and crashes far and centred; no audio error.

V. RESULTS

    32 cues; client tests 9 (the FM operator against J0, Nyquist,
    the decimator passing 1 kHz and stopping 30 kHz, the far take, the
    seamless loop, placement on and off screen to the floor). make test
    passes; no warnings.

    Band energy, per cent, <150 / 150-600 / 600-2.5k / 2.5-8k / >8k Hz:

      gun (cannon blast)   71  27   1   1   0
      cannon (gunshot)     10  16  23  24  28
      strike (armour)       4  31  43  19   3
      breech               19  22   5  22  32
      track                22  41  22  13   2

VI. DISCUSSION

    The three weapon sounds are now three different places in the
    spectrum, which is most of what makes them three sounds. The fog
    rule is the one design decision Paul's model did not settle: a
    sound everywhere on the map would have told a player where hidden
    drones die. Heard from nowhere, it is background and no more.

VII. OPEN PROBLEMS

    1. Still nobody has listened. make sounds, then a match.
    2. The tracks are the player's own only; other tanks on screen make
       no engine noise.
    3. The capture clock and the track loop are state, not events; a
       replay or a seat that joins mid-capture hears them from the next
       snapshot.

VIII. NEXT STEPS
